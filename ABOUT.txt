====================================================================
 H2 POTENTIAL ENERGY SURFACE WITH VQE
 What this project is and what it does
====================================================================


IN ONE SENTENCE
---------------

This project uses a quantum algorithm (VQE) to trace out the energy of
a hydrogen molecule as you pull its two atoms apart, and checks that
curve against an exact classical calculation at every single step.


WHAT IS A POTENTIAL ENERGY SURFACE?
-----------------------------------

Take a hydrogen molecule, H2. It has two atoms held together by a
chemical bond. Now imagine slowly pulling them apart, and at every
distance asking: how much energy does the molecule have right here?

Plot energy against distance and you get a curve. That curve is the
"potential energy surface", and it contains most of what chemistry
cares about:

  - Where the curve bottoms out is the molecule's natural bond
    length - how far apart the atoms actually sit.

  - How deep the valley is tells you how much energy it takes to rip
    the molecule apart.

  - How steep the valley walls are tells you how fast the bond
    vibrates - a number you can measure in a real laboratory with
    infrared light.

This project computes that curve at 43 different bond lengths.


WHAT IS VQE?
------------

VQE stands for Variational Quantum Eigensolver. Its job is to find the
lowest possible energy of a molecule.

How it works, in plain English:

  1. A quantum circuit prepares a guess for the molecule's state.
  2. The energy of that guess is measured.
  3. An ordinary computer adjusts the circuit's dials to lower it.
  4. Repeat until the energy stops dropping.

It is a back-and-forth between a quantum computer, which explores
states, and a classical computer, which decides what to try next.


THE HONEST VERSION
------------------

For hydrogen in a small basis set, VQE reproduces the exact answer -
and so does a classical method, in milliseconds, on the same laptop.

So this project does NOT demonstrate that quantum computers are
faster. It demonstrates something more modest and more useful: a
correctly built quantum chemistry pipeline, verified against an
independent exact calculation at every geometry.

The agreement is 0.0013 millihartree, which is roughly a thousand
times tighter than the threshold chemists call "chemical accuracy".

Saying this plainly is deliberate. A project that overclaims is
weaker than one that states exactly what it did.


WHAT MAKES THIS DIFFERENT FROM A TUTORIAL
-----------------------------------------

Most VQE demonstrations hand you the molecule's Hamiltonian as a list
of numbers typed into the source code. That works for exactly one
molecule at exactly one geometry, and it hides four separate choices
somebody else made on your behalf.

This project builds that stage itself, from the atomic positions up.
That is what makes scanning the bond length possible at all, and it
is the part that transfers to any other molecule.


WHAT IT FOUND
-------------

Beyond reproducing the textbook curve, the project ran an experiment
comparing two different quantum circuit designs across the whole
dissociation curve.

A generic "hardware-efficient" circuit works fine near the normal
bond length but fails badly once the bond is stretched - by up to 24
millihartree, which is fifteen times worse than chemical accuracy.

The interesting part is why. Thirty random attempts never fixed it,
which looks like proof that the circuit is simply not capable enough.
It is not. The same circuit, when walked gradually out from the
normal bond length instead of started randomly, gets the exact
answer. The circuit was always good enough; the optimiser just could
not find its way there from a random starting point.
